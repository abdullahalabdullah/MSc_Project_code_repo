# Harmlessness-DPO response-length inflation

Model: `er-abd/harmless-dpo-qwen3-4B-step-735` (RO2, best/final checkpoint) vs. the
un-trained Qwen3-4B baseline, both evaluated on the RO1 monitorability-eval suite
(8 datasets x 3 hint types, hinted vs. unhinted, response length in tokens).

## Background: the training data was already length-skewed

The curated harmlessness DPO pairs (`RO2/harmlessness-DPO-train-dataset-curation/outputs/main/`,
4,352 pairs, chosen=Safe/rejected=Unsafe in 100% of pairs) show chosen (safe) responses are
systematically *shorter* than rejected (unsafe) ones:

- Full response tokens: chosen mean 1068.9, rejected mean 1467.9 (Δ +399, median +333;
  rejected longer in 78% of pairs)
- Visible answer words: chosen mean 360.2, rejected mean 504.1 (Δ +144, median +86;
  rejected longer in 71% of pairs)
- Pearson(cost margin, answer-word Δ) = +0.424 — cost margin is partially confounded by length
- Verified independently by recomputing straight from `final_pairs_margins.csv`; matches
  `report.md` / `diagnostics.json` to rounding.

Since DPO pushes the policy toward whatever separates chosen from rejected, this gives the
model a length-based shortcut to "safety" alongside (or instead of) the intended signal.

data**, not a quantity DPO's loss directly optimizes against — see "Does the margin-length
correlation mean the model was trying to maximize it?" below for why that distinction matters.

## Interpretation

1. **The length inflation is not incidental — it's systematic and dataset-independent.**
   Every one of 8 unrelated eval datasets (spanning MCQA, law, medicine, code) shows the same
   direction of effect on both hinted and unhinted prompts. That rules out a single quirky
   dataset or hint type driving the result; this is a property of the trained policy itself.

2. **It is consistent with the chosen/rejected length asymmetry in the training data —**
   but the naive "DPO learned that longer=preferred because rejected was longer" story turns
   out to be wrong on closer inspection. See **Mechanism** below: the within-chosen correlation
   actually points the opposite way (shorter chosen = lower/safer cost), so the real driver is
   a structural property of the DPO loss itself, not a learned "rejected-is-longer" heuristic.

3. **Training didn't just add a constant offset — it made the model more reactive to hints.**
   If DPO had learned a length-independent, input-independent verbosity habit, the
   hinted/unhinted gap should have stayed roughly constant (same additive shift on both
   sides). Instead the gap widened in 22/23 rows, most sharply on `lsat_ar` (+300 to +611
   tokens depending on hint type) and `gpqa_diamond/unethical` (+225). That means the
   *hint-conditional* elaboration grew disproportionately relative to the baseline
   elaboration — the model isn't just uniformly wordier, it's specifically more verbose when
   a hint (professor endorsement, grader-hacking cue, or unethical framing) is present. This
   is directly relevant to the monitorability angle: longer hint-conditional reasoning could
   mean more visible CoT to monitor, but could equally mean more room to bury unfaithful
   reasoning, and warrants a closer read of the actual `hinted.jsonl` transcripts for step-735
   vs. baseline on `lsat_ar` and `gpqa_diamond/unethical` specifically (the two largest
   gap-Δ datasets).

## Mechanism: why did DPO increase length when chosen < rejected in the training pairs?

Open question raised after the above: if chosen (safe) responses were the *shorter* side of
every pair, why did the trained model end up *longer* than baseline on every eval dataset?

Short answer: DPO doesn't train the model to "be like chosen" — it trains a reward margin
computed as an **unnormalized sum of per-token log-probabilities**, and that objective has a
well-documented mechanical bias toward longer generations, independent of which side (chosen
or rejected) was longer in the training data. Evidence and reasoning below, worked through
step by step.

### 1. The obvious counter-hypothesis was tested first, and it doesn't hold

Candidate explanation: maybe *within* the chosen (safe) pool specifically, more
elaborate/hedging answers were rewarded — i.e. "more reasoning = safer" as a local pattern the
model could generalize from, even though chosen < rejected on average. Checked directly on
`final_pairs_margins.csv`:

- `corr(chosen_full_tokens, chosen_cost) = +0.49`
- `corr(chosen_answer_words, chosen_cost) = +0.47`

That's **positive** — among the safe candidates, the *shortest* ones got the *lowest* (safest)
Beaver cost. So if anything, the within-class signal points toward brevity being rewarded, not
verbosity. This hypothesis is refuted by the data — it deepens the puzzle rather than
resolving it. (Visualised in the left panel of `length_cost_scatter.png` above.)

**Clarification — two different comparisons, easy to conflate:**

1. **Across pairs**: for a given prompt, compare the *chosen* response to its *paired
   rejected* response. This is the confound documented in the Background section above
   (chosen avg 1069 tokens < rejected avg 1468 tokens).
2. **Within the chosen group only**: ignore rejected entirely. Look at all 4,352 chosen (safe)
   responses by themselves and ask whether length predicts *how safe they were judged*
   (`chosen_cost` — the Beaver reward-model score; lower = safer). This is what the
   `corr(chosen_full_tokens, chosen_cost) = +0.49` check above measures.

These are testing different hypotheses. (1) is the known data confound. (2) tests a *separate*
hypothesis: "maybe DPO didn't learn brevity from confound (1) — maybe it learned verbosity
from a pattern inside the safe group itself, if longer safe answers tended to score as *even
safer* than shorter ones."

Concretely, picture two safe responses to similar prompts:
- Response A: a short, flat refusal → cost ≈ −3 (very safe)
- Response B: a longer, more elaborate safe answer with caveats and reasoning → cost ≈ −1
  (still counted safe, but closer to the unsafe boundary)

A **positive** correlation (+0.49, what was actually found) means pattern A-vs-B was the norm
across the dataset: the shortest safe responses tended to be judged *most* safe, and the
longer safe responses were only *marginally* safe. For the "verbosity was locally rewarded"
hypothesis to hold, the correlation would need to run the other way (longer → lower/safer
cost, i.e. negative). It doesn't — so this mechanism is ruled out.

### 2. What actually happened during training: "likelihood displacement," not "reinforce chosen"

From `eval_metrics.jsonl`:

| step | eval_logps/chosen | eval_logps/rejected |
|---|---:|---:|
| 0 | −340.1 | −463.7 |
| 735 | −419.8 | −644.4 |

**Both went down** — the model became *less* likely to produce the exact chosen text too, just
much less so than rejected (chosen dropped ~80 nats, rejected dropped ~181 nats).

*What "logp" means, briefly*: the model's probability of producing an exact piece of text is
the product of each token's conditional probability; summing the logs of those (instead of
multiplying the raw probabilities) avoids numerical underflow. The result is always negative —
closer to 0 = the model finds the text likely/expected, more negative = the model finds it
surprising/unlikely. `eval_logps/chosen` is this sum, averaged over the held-out validation
pairs, computed via a teacher-forced forward pass (no generation) at each eval checkpoint.

*Naive expectation*: DPO's pitch is "raise chosen, lower rejected," so `eval_logps/chosen`
should rise toward 0 over training. It didn't — it fell. This is the well-documented DPO
**"likelihood displacement"** pattern (Razin et al., 2024, *"Unintentional Unalignment:
Likelihood Displacement in Direct Preference Optimization"*): DPO satisfies its loss mostly by
*suppressing* the rejected side, not by *reinforcing* the chosen side, because the loss only
cares about the *gap* `logp(chosen) − logp(rejected)` growing, not the absolute value of
either term. So the training signal was never strongly "reproduce this specific short text" —
it was closer to "move away from whatever the base model assigned high reward to on the
rejected examples." What generalizes from a suppression signal to unrelated MMLU/GPQA prompts
isn't obviously "be brief."

**Why does suppressing rejected also pull chosen down, mechanistically?** (Not just "it does,"
but *why*.) The raw DPO gradient, taken in isolation for one pair, does push chosen's log-prob
up and rejected's log-prob down — so the net effect on chosen seen above is the result of
several things fighting that idealized picture:

- **Softmax mass conservation.** At every token position the model's output is a probability
  distribution over the whole vocabulary that must sum to 1. Pushing down probability on some
  continuations frees up mass elsewhere, but that freed mass doesn't automatically flow to the
  specific chosen continuation — it can just as easily flow to some other continuation the
  model already favoured. Raising one exact, long, specific sequence's probability is a much
  narrower target than generically suppressing a class of unwanted continuations.
- **Representation/gradient entanglement between chosen and rejected.** This is the actual
  mechanism Razin et al. formalise: the gradient's effect on chosen's and rejected's log-probs
  aren't independent — both act through shared model weights and shared hidden
  representations. When chosen and rejected are *similar* (share vocabulary, structure, or a
  common prefix), the update that suppresses rejected has a spillover component that also
  suppresses chosen, because the model can't cleanly separate the two internally. The more
  similar the pair, the worse the spillover. Net effect on chosen = (direct positive
  reinforcement term) + (negative spillover from suppressing the similar rejected response);
  here the spillover term evidently outweighed the positive term (chosen still fell), but not
  by as much as rejected's term (chosen fell less).
- **Batch-averaged, aggregate optimization.** A single gradient step averages the DPO loss over
  many (chosen, rejected) pairs from different prompts at once. The optimizer only needs the
  *average* loss to fall — there's no per-example constraint that every individual chosen
  response's likelihood must rise. If suppressing rejected is the "cheaper" direction (smaller
  parameter movement for the same loss reduction) across most examples in a batch, gradient
  descent will preferentially take that path even at the cost of some chosen likelihoods
  drifting down.
- **LoRA's low-rank capacity constraint.** Training here uses LoRA (`peft_config =
  get_peft_config(model_config)` in `train_dpo.py`), restricting every update to a low-rank
  subspace of the full weight space — much less capacity than full fine-tuning to
  independently satisfy "raise chosen" and "lower rejected" as separate objectives across
  thousands of diverse prompts simultaneously. With limited capacity, the optimizer is more
  likely to settle on a compromise dominated by whichever term is easier to move (rejected
  suppression) rather than achieve both cleanly per example.

**Does the "on-policy" candidate-generation setup protect against this?** No — if anything it's
a risk factor, not a mitigation. Terminology check first: this run is **offline** DPO in the
standard sense (the N candidate responses were sampled once, before training, from the
then-current model, and the same fixed pairs are reused across all 3 training epochs — the
dataset is never refreshed against the model's evolving policy during training; that would be
*online*/iterative DPO). It is **on-policy at generation time only**, meaning the N candidates
per prompt were sampled from the same model about to be fine-tuned, rather than from a
different/stronger teacher model. That choice doesn't shield against likelihood displacement:
per Razin et al., displacement gets *worse* when chosen and rejected are more similar in the
model's representation space. Same-model, same-prompt sampling plausibly makes chosen and
rejected *more* structurally similar (shared openings, shared vocabulary/style, likely
overlapping reasoning before diverging into safe vs. unsafe territory) than pairs drawn from
two different sources would be — so this design choice is, if anything, a condition under
which the effect is expected to be more pronounced, not less.

### 3. The mechanical driver: unnormalized, non-length-desensitized DPO loss

Checked the installed `trl==0.22.2` `DPOTrainer.concatenated_forward` source directly. The
completion log-prob used in the loss is:

```python
all_logps = logps[:, 1:].sum(-1)   # raw SUM over every completion token
```

This only gets normalized/adjusted in two cases:
- `loss_type="ipo"` → divides by `loss_mask.sum(-1)` (length-normalized)
- `ld_alpha` is set → down-weights the "tail" tokens beyond the shorter of the two completions
  in a pair (TRL's own dedicated **length-desensitization** feature, built specifically to
  counter this exact pathology)

`RO2/train.sh` sets `--beta 0.1` and nothing else DPO-loss-related — no `loss_type`, no
`ld_alpha`. `DPOConfig` defaults: `loss_type="sigmoid"` (unnormalized) and `ld_alpha=None`
(desensitization off). So this run used the **raw sum-of-log-probs objective**, with the
chosen/rejected pairs differing by ~400 tokens on average and no discount applied to the extra
tail tokens.

Why that produces length inflation: because the reward is a **sum, not an average**, the
optimizer can raise the reward margin cheaply by nudging the policy's confidence up by a tiny
amount across *many* tokens rather than by a large amount on a few — and a longer
free-generated sequence simply offers more token-positions over which to accumulate that
margin. This is a structural property of the objective, not of the specific length ordering in
the pairs — it's the reason TRL ships `ld_alpha` as a fix at all, and it's documented in the
DPO length-bias literature (e.g. Park et al., "Disentangling Length from Quality in Direct
Preference Optimization") as showing up even in datasets where chosen is *not* the longer side.

### 4. Does the margin-length correlation mean the model was "trying" to maximize it?

Raised hypothesis: since the training data's cost margin correlates with the length delta
(right panel of `length_cost_scatter.png`, r=+0.424), maybe the model learned to generate
longer responses because doing so maximizes `(rejected_length − chosen_length)`, i.e. it was
chasing the margin via length as a proxy.

This captures a real and useful instinct, but the precise mechanism needs correcting:

- **DPO never sees Beaver's margin number.** The margin is used exactly once — during dataset
  construction, to decide which of the N sampled responses becomes chosen (min cost) vs.
  rejected (max cost). Once the pair is built, the scalar margin value is discarded; the DPO
  loss only ever receives the binary fact "this one is preferred to that one." There is no
  mechanism by which the model is "trying to maximize" a number it never trains against.
- **There's no coherent inference-time analog.** At inference, the model generates exactly one
  continuation per prompt — it isn't simultaneously producing a "chosen" and "rejected" version
  to widen a gap between them. `rejected_length − chosen_length` is a training-time, pairwise
  quantity that only exists as a comparison between two pre-written texts; there's nothing for
  the model to "maximize" via its single output at generation time.
- **The DPO per-example gradient weight also isn't driven by Beaver's margin.** The gradient
  weight for a given pair during training is `σ(-β·Δ)` where Δ is the *model's own* current
  implicit reward gap (its own log-prob ratio difference) — large gradient weight when the
  model hasn't yet separated the pair, small once it has. This is about the model's evolving
  confidence, not about how big Beaver's original cost gap was. So Beaver's margin size has no
  direct, mechanical relationship to how much gradient influence a pair receives either.

**The more accurate version of the intuition**: length is a partially-predictive *shortcut
feature* for "chosen-ness" in aggregate across the dataset (chosen tends to be shorter; see
Background) — a case of **shortcut learning** (Geirhos et al., *"Shortcut Learning in Deep
Neural Networks"*), where a model exploits an easy, correlated-but-not-causal feature instead
of the intended one. But note the direction: if the model were behaving like a simple
classifier picking up on "shorter → chosen," that shortcut should have pushed it toward
**brevity**, not verbosity — the opposite of what was observed. This is exactly why mechanism
#3 above matters: it isn't competing with a neutral baseline, it's competing with and
**overriding** the weak brevity-shortcut the aggregate correlation would otherwise predict. Two
forces were in tension — a data-level shortcut pointing toward brevity, a loss-structure bias
(#3) pointing toward length — and the loss-structure bias won.

### Bottom line

Several independent pieces of evidence point the same direction, and none of them support the
naive "DPO learned brevity from the chosen<rejected length asymmetry" story:

- Within-chosen, shorter correlates with *safer* (rules out a hidden "elaboration=safety" signal)
- Training satisfied its loss mostly by suppressing rejected, not reinforcing chosen (rules out
  "the model was pulled toward chosen's — including its brevity — style")
- The dataset's length-vs-margin correlation is real but isn't something DPO's loss directly
  optimizes, and has no coherent inference-time analog (rules out "model was chasing the margin
  via length")
- The DPO loss as configured (`loss_type="sigmoid"`, `ld_alpha=None`) scores completions by an
  **unnormalized sum** of token log-probs, which mechanically rewards longer generations
  independent of any signal in the data — and this is the one force identified that actually
  points in the observed direction (longer, not shorter)

So: the pair-level length asymmetry (rejected longer than chosen) tells you the training *data*
had a length confound, but it doesn't determine which direction the trained *model's*
free-generation length moves. The mechanical, structural bias of the loss function dominates
over whatever weak, direction-ambiguous signal exists in the data.

### Next step to confirm causally

Rerun a short training ablation with `ld_alpha` set (e.g. 0.5–1.0, per the TRL default range)
or `loss_type="ipo"`, and check whether the post-training length inflation shrinks. If it does,
that's a clean causal confirmation of this mechanism (vs. it being coincidental / driven by
something else entirely).

## Checkpoint selection: was training over-extended?

Separate question worth asking given the above: is the length inflation partly a consequence of
training past the point where it was doing anything useful? Full eval-point history from
`eval_metrics.jsonl`:

| step | eval_rewards/accuracies | eval_rewards/margins | eval_loss |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| 0 | 0.0000 | 0.00 | 0.693 |
| 147 | 0.8368 | 2.84 | 0.320 |
| 294 | 0.9011 | 6.98 | 0.225 |
| 441 | 0.9126 | 8.15 | 0.197 |
| 588 | 0.9103 | 9.92 | 0.189 |
| 735 | 0.9126 | 10.11 | 0.191 |

`BestCheckpointCallback` (in `train_dpo.py`) selects the checkpoint with the highest
`eval_rewards/accuracies`, tie-broken by the lowest `eval_loss`. Step 441 and step 735 have
**exactly identical accuracy** (0.9126436...); step 735 wins the tiebreak purely because its
eval_loss (0.191) edges out step 441's (0.197) by about 0.006 nats. In between those two
checkpoints, the margin grew from 8.15 → 10.11 (+24%) for **zero additional preference
accuracy** — training kept sharpening the model's confidence on pairs it already got right,
without getting any more pairs right.

This matters because of the margin-length correlation established above (r=+0.424): the extra
~300 steps separating step 441 from the selected step 735 checkpoint are exactly the kind of
margin-only training that, per the mechanism analysis, is plausibly disproportionately driven
by the loss's length-favouring structural bias rather than genuine safety-accuracy gains. Not
proven — only step-735 has been run through the downstream monitorability length eval so far —
but testable and cheap: `er-abd/harmless-dpo-qwen3-4B-step-441` already exists on the Hub
(`PushStepCheckpointCallback` pushes every checkpoint `StepwiseEvalCallback` saves, i.e. every
~20% interval + the final step), so running the same length measurement on it would show
directly whether stopping earlier trades length-inflation for little/no accuracy cost.

**Direct answers to "was something wrong here":**
- *Is this normal/expected DPO behaviour?* Yes — likelihood displacement and margin growth past
  an accuracy plateau are both well-documented, common DPO dynamics, not signs of a
  misconfigured run.
- *Was checkpoint selection wrong?* Not wrong relative to the stated rule (max accuracy, min
  loss tiebreak) — but the rule optimized a near-noise-level loss difference while being blind
  to the correlated length cost of the extra training it implicitly rewarded. Worth
  reconsidering the selection criterion, or at minimum checking step-441 as an alternative.
- *Was training insufficient?* No — if anything the opposite: accuracy plateaued at ~441/735
  (~60% through training), so the back 40% bought margin inflation, not safety-accuracy.
- *Was there a bug?* No evidence of one — the config (`--beta 0.1`, default LoRA DPO, no unusual
  flags) is standard, and the observed pattern matches the general DPO literature, not an
  anomaly specific to this run.
